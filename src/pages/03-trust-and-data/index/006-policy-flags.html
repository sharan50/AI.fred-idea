6The policy flag table and the lobbying agenda

Full delegation, identity acts included, is the designed ceiling; the flag table is what the policy layer reads at runtime to decide, per jurisdiction and act type, whether an act is delegated, bounces to the user's device, or is prohibited pending a regulatory answer (DR-003). Table 3.5 records the designed flag, the ceiling, and every cell is a legal item; the pilot's runtime setting is narrower, as 07 records: for identity acts outside India the loaded flag is bounce until counsel's answer sets it, so the UK and US identity rows below are the ceiling and not the pilot's value, and for the act make a payment under the cap the pilot's loaded flag is bounce in every market, so the debit is the user's own act on their device until a rail admits a registered agent, the Unified Agent Protocol row of Table 3.4 being the first candidate. Detracting is a flag; adding would be a rebuild. A bounce line wins even where the user ticked the identity act in the envelope; a prohibited pending line closes the step refused-policy before anyone attempts it and carries a legal item in 07. The table ships in each market's legal pack and is signed before a pilot opens (06). It doubles as the lobbying agenda: for every line that is not delegated, the last two columns say what to ask the regulator and what the user gains if the answer is yes. A flag is one of three values, delegated, bounce or prohibited pending, and the lookup has one interface: the policy layer is keyed by jurisdiction and act type, the act-type rows below (the table's last row is the basis those acts rest on, not a key), and every step type in the catalogue 02 owns carries exactly one act type and no flag of its own, the flag living in the policy layer alone, so the classifier, the envelope filter and the substitutor all ask the same question with the same key; a row that does not exist evaluates to bounce. Where a line carries a condition, it is a conjunction of named predicates from a fixed list (the portal is non-financial; the login, the consent or the fact is in the envelope; a mandate the user set up names the payee; a network token bound to this task and its cap exists for the merchant), evaluated by the policy layer from the envelope record and the adapter's facts at classification time and again from the substitutor's context at placement, never a free expression; a delegated whose condition does not hold reads bounce. The alias class and the surface are not part of the key: the substitutor checks them after the flag (1.1). Every flag is a legal item to verify, not a design constraint, and is chipped so. A one-time code has no line in the table: it is the user's factor by design, {{otp}} is never an alias and no flag is consulted for it (the classifier in 02; 5.1). The jurisdiction in the key is the delegator's: for the user's own aliases the user's jurisdiction, the market on the request; for a dependant's aliases the jurisdiction her delegation record carries, so an identity act on her aliases is flagged under it and never the user's. For an act toward an institution the same lookup is made twice, once under the delegator's jurisdiction and once under the institution's market, and the stricter result governs, prohibited pending over bounce over delegated, a row missing in either evaluating to bounce, so an India-market user's identity act on a UK institution reads the UK row and bounces until counsel's answer sets that flag; the rail is the institution's market's, and the signed step context names both jurisdictions the lookup used, so the substitutor checks the flag under the same keys (2.1; 01, 1.5; DR-003).
