04Operations

A worker on our floor executes one step at a time from a card that names the client as a number and every sensitive value as an alias. Three tiers, three shifts on one 24-hour wheel, a room built to be attested, and QA as a duty on every shift are what turn that card into the promise: the work gets done, and no person saw the value.

1Task cards and pseudonymisation

A worker never receives a task. They receive a step, on a card the planner wrote, and the card carries the least the step needs: a pseudonym for the client, the aliases the system will place, the facts in clear the script refers to, the script itself, what a good outcome looks like, and a structured result to fill in. Everything else about the task and the user stays where it is. The console that renders the card is specified in 02, worker console; this section says how a card comes to exist and what it may contain.

1.1How a card is generated

A card is the planner's output for one step whose class is human. The planner produces the plan; the classifier marks each step ai, human or user; for every human step the card generator assembles a card from the step record: the surface, the aliases the substitutor may place there, the facts in clear, the script, the expected outcome, and the result schema the step's likely outcome codes require. A need-to-know filter runs before the card is issued. It drops any fact the script does not reference, drops any alias the policy layer does not permit on that surface in that jurisdiction (and returns the step for re-classification), and replaces the user's identity with the task's pseudonym. Only then does the card enter the routing rules of section 3.

Cards are generated when a step becomes ready, not in advance: step three's outcome may change the plan, and a card that exists is a card that can be read. When a plan is revised, the cards of superseded steps leave the console at the moment the steps move to cancelled.

1.2Need-to-know and Client 4471

The pseudonym is issued per task and is stable for every step of that task. Two cards from two tasks by the same user carry two different numbers, so a worker cannot join them; the mapping from pseudonym to user lives in the task service and appears on no card, in no console view and in no log the floor can read. Within a task the number stays put, so a pod carrying a five-step application can follow the thread. "Client 4471" is the pattern: a word and a number, nothing that hints at a name, a city or a vertical.

Need-to-know applies at the field level, not the document level. A worker booking a consultation learns the hospital, the department and the acceptable window; not the diagnosis, the referral letter, or the fact that the same client opened a bank account last week. Underneath sits the alias rule from 03: everything the vault classes as sensitive is only ever an alias, so a card cannot carry a real value even by mistake, and the filter governs the non-sensitive remainder. One-time passcodes are never ours at all: {{otp}} is not an alias, and a step that needs one bounces to the user's own device. The regulator asks the same of the customer; the Reserve Bank of India cautioned the public in August 2024 not to share login details, copies of KYC documents, card details, PINs, passwords or OTPs with unidentified persons or agencies verified [1], and our floor is built so that a worker could not receive one even if a user tried to send it.

1.4The script and the result

The script is numbered, written in the second person, and marks the moments at which the system places a value. The worker cues placement; the substitutor places the value directly into the surface; the worker's stream shows dots or a "value placed" panel; the access ledger records the placement in the user's words. Wording is verbatim wherever it carries consequence, above all in what we may and may not agree to.

The result is structured. The worker selects one outcome code from the fixed vocabulary in the worker console and fills the fields that code requires: a reference for ok, a variance note for ok-with-variance, a party and a response window for blocked-third-party. Free text is bounded to the variance note. Every field is validated on entry against the vault's known-value hashes; a match is rejected and flagged to QA, so a value a counterpart happened to read aloud cannot be written down. The card has no channel to the user: a question closes the step as blocked-needs-user, which the planner turns into a user step.

2The 24/7 shift design

One office runs the clock. The day shift covers India; two night shifts cover the west, first the UK and Europe and then the US. The three shifts turn on a single 24-hour wheel in Indian Standard Time, and the moment we design hardest for is the handover between them. This is operational design with no cost model attached; pool sizing against the wheel is one of the three inputs that 05 defers.

2.1The wheel

Read the wheel from the outside in. The dial is Indian Standard Time, midnight at the top. The outer band holds the three shifts; where two bands overlap, the accent marks a handover window. The arcs inside are each market's working day in Indian time; Table 4.2 gives the numbers. Every shift boundary falls inside some market's working day, which is why the handover has to be a designed event rather than a change of faces.

2.2The offsets used

Time zone offsets are facts, and we state the ones drawn. Indian Standard Time is UTC+5:30 all year; India keeps no summer time. The UK is UTC+0 in winter and UTC+1 in summer. US Eastern is UTC-5 in winter and UTC-4 in summer; US Pacific is UTC-8 and UTC-7. Central European Time is UTC+1 and UTC+2. The wheel is drawn on summer offsets, where the year stands as this page is revised; in winter every western arc moves an hour later on the dial and the shift windows absorb it without redrawing. The working day drawn is a plain nine to five-thirty, not a claim about any institution's opening hours.

2.3The three shifts and the handover

Each shift carries the same complement of roles: an L1 pool, L2 cover for the verticals whose markets are open, at least one L3 seat, a QA owner and escalation cover. The boundaries in Table 4.3 are illustrative; what is fixed is the shape, one day shift for India and two night shifts for the west, and the rule that every shift overlaps the next.

The handover is the accent of the figure because it is where a floor is most likely to drop something. Thirty minutes of overlap, illustrative, in which the outgoing QA owner walks the incoming one through the exceptions board item by item; every card in running or checking is either closed by its holder before they leave or explicitly re-assigned with its timer intact; and the L3 seat passes with the list of steps in checking. Nothing is handed over by leaving it on a screen. The state machine makes this enforceable rather than hopeful: a step whose holder has left without closing or re-assigning it still has a live timer, the timer expires on the incoming shift, and the watchdog puts the task on the board.

3Tiers, escalation and dual control

Three tiers, and two functions that stand outside them. L1 is a generalist pool that executes reversible steps. L2 is six specialist pods, one per vertical, for the steps whose script needs familiarity with an institution's ways. L3 is super-trust: the workers who execute delegated identity acts and who hold the human seat in dual control. QA and escalation are standalone functions rather than tiers, and they report outside the tier line so that the people who check the work do not answer to the people who route it.

3.1The three tiers

L1, the generalist pool
Executes reversible steps in any vertical: a call to confirm an appointment, a form filled from aliases, a status enquiry, a document request. L1 never fires an irreversible step. Every worker starts here, and its cards are the most fully scripted.
L2, the specialist pods
Six pods, one for each vertical: banking and wealth; legal and administrative; identity and security; health and medical; family affairs; travel. A pod takes the steps that carry its vertical's tag, the steps L1 escalates inside its vertical, and the irreversible human steps in its vertical, which it executes under dual control with the AI as the checking party. A pod knows how its institutions behave: which desk to ask for, which forms bounce, which fees are negotiable.
L3, super-trust
The smallest tier and the most vetted. L3 executes the delegated identity acts the policy layer permits in a jurisdiction (presenting a document, stating an identity number, answering security questions from the context store, completing a KYC form), always with the value placed by the substitutor and never seen. L3 also holds the human seat in dual control whenever the AI is the executor of an irreversible step. L3 workers are drawn from L2 after a tenure and a further check, and an L3 seat is staffed on every shift.

3.2Where a step goes

Routing is a rule, not a judgement, and it runs when the card is issued. A step goes to L1 unless one of three things is true. If it carries a vertical tag, it goes to that vertical's pod; the planner sets the tag from the task's taxonomy and the pod's own escalations refine it. If it is a delegated identity act, it goes to L3. If it is irreversible and inside the envelope, it goes to L2 or L3, enters checking before it fires, and never goes to L1. A worker cannot pull a card from a tier above their own; a pod can push a card down to L1 once the specialist part is done.

3.3Dual control on the floor

For an irreversible step inside the envelope, whichever party executes, the other checks before it fires. That is dual control, fixed in DR-004 and specified for the console in 02, and on the floor it has two shapes. When the AI executes, an L3 worker checks: the console shows the step, the envelope lines it touches, the expected outcome and the exact act about to fire, with aliases in place of values, and the L3 confirms or refuses with a reason; the substitutor will not place a value for an irreversible step until that confirmation is on the step record. When a worker executes, the AI performs the same check and the worker sees its result before acting; and because the executor is L2 or L3, never L1, the person at the keyboard is one we have vetted for that vertical or that act. Both identities go on the step record and into the user's ledger in plain words; a step that stays in checking past its timer goes to the board.

3.4Escalation

Any worker can escalate any card, and a card escalated is not a card failed. The escalation desk is a standalone function staffed on every shift by people drawn from L2 and L3 who are not, during that shift, executing cards of their own. It receives cards marked blocked, unclear or refused and does one of four things: re-routes the card to a pod or to L3; returns it to the planner for a re-plan, closing the step as failed-final with the alternatives considered; confirms a refusal under policy, closing it as refused-policy with the policy reference; or bounces a question to the user through blocked-needs-user. The desk never edits a card in place, because a card is the planner's record of what was asked. Escalations are counted per card template, and a template that keeps producing them is a template QA rewrites.

4Site controls as part of the trust guarantee

The room is part of the promise. We say that no person on our side sees a real value; the software makes that true on the screen, and the site controls make it true around the screen. Office-mandatory, 24/7 operation is a fixed decision recorded in DR-006, and the controls below are why it is a trust decision rather than a facilities preference. Each closes a path in the threat model in 03 that software alone cannot close: the path from a screen to a camera, a notebook, a second device or a memory.

4.2What a visitor would see

A quiet floor. Rows of identical workstations, each showing a card: a client number, a numbered script, aliases in braces, and, where a form is on screen, fields rendered as dots. Workers on headsets who fall silent for a moment while a panel reads "value placed", because their microphone is muted while the system speaks. No paper anywhere. A wall display carrying the exceptions board for the shift, by client number and state, with a named owner at the top. A glass-walled zone at one end where the L3 seats and the escalation desk sit, with two people in it at any time. Lockers at the door, and the visitor's own phone inside one of them. What the visitor would not see is a single real value, on any screen, at any station, and that is the point of the tour.

4.3Why remote is not permitted now

Remote work is not permitted because a home cannot be attested. The guarantee "no person saw it" is made by the room as much as by the software: a home has a family, a camera in a laptop lid, a second screen, a phone on the desk and a network we do not run. The screen shows only aliases and dots, so the value itself is protected wherever the worker sits; what a home cannot give us is the ability to say so to an auditor, a regulator or a user with evidence. Remote-safe operation is a designed-later programme, parked with an owner and a trigger in 07, not a maybe. It will need a remote station we own, a room we can attest by other means, and a legal pack per jurisdiction saying what a home worker may process; until it exists, every card is executed on the floor.

The alternative is what the field does today. Hulp gives each family a pod of three assistants and says that anything which books, pays or goes out is reviewed by a real human first verified [2]. Time etc recommends password managers so that an assistant can use a client's account without seeing the password verified [3], which hides the password and nothing behind it. Both put a person in the loop; neither puts a boundary between the person and the value, and neither describes a room. Our floor is the boundary made physical.

5QA and the exceptions board

QA is a shift duty with a named owner, not a department that reads reports afterwards. On every shift one person owns the exceptions board, samples closed steps against the redacted artefacts, and follows up the verdicts that came in. The board itself is specified in 02, worker console; this section says whose it is and what they do with it.

5.1Who owns the board

The QA owner on shift owns the board, and that ownership is the first line of the shift roster. The watchdog puts a task on the board when it has no live timer, when a step has sat in checking past its timer, or when a blocked step has outrun its response window; the owner's job is to give every item a person and a timer before the shift ends, and to hand over whatever cannot be resolved as the first item of the handover. The board is never emptied by fiat: an item leaves it only through a recorded transition, and the invariant in 02 is what the owner is enforcing by hand. The owner also holds the shift's escalation counts and the list of results the validator rejected, since a rejected result is a near-miss on the one thing the floor must never do.

5.2Sampling closed steps

Every shift the QA owner draws a sample of closed steps, stratified by tier, vertical and outcome code, weighted toward ok-with-variance, failed-final and refused-policy, and toward any worker in their first weeks on a tier. Each sampled step is judged against its redacted artefacts, the only artefacts anyone can view under 03's redaction rules and DR-009: the masked screenshot, the transcript with its muted windows, the DOM capture with dots where a value went. A finding is one of four kinds: the outcome code was wrong for what happened; the script was followed and the outcome was still wrong; the script was right and not followed; or the card itself was defective, by a need-to-know breach, a missing fact or the wrong surface. Findings go to the card templates, the routing rules and the training queue, in that order, because a defective card explains more failures than a careless worker does.

5.3Verdict follow-up

The verdict is the user's and it is the ground truth of quality; the outcome codes are not, as 01 sets out. QA reads every wrong-outcome and could-not-complete verdict against its step trace before the following shift ends, and a sample of done-rough. The reading is not a grade for the worker; it is a search for the step, the card or the route that produced the verdict, and it ends in a finding of the same four kinds. Verdict joined to step trace is how every product change is judged, per task type, and QA is where that joining is done by hand until the harness does it in bulk. Whatever make-good policy the founder settles, an open item in 07, QA's reading of the trace is the evidence it will rest on.

6Service levels, stated qualitatively

We state service levels in words on purpose. Response numbers are a pilot's output, not its input, and tying them to pool size now would smuggle in the economics that 05 defers. What can be fixed before a pilot is the shape of each promise and the mechanism that keeps it: every level below is carried by a timer in the state machine, so a missed level is an exception on the board rather than a statistic discovered later.

Table 4.5 Service levels as shapes and mechanisms; any number shown is an illustrative default, not a commitment.
MomentWhat we promiseHow the floor keeps it
AcknowledgementEvery request is acknowledged on receipt, in minutes rather than hours, on the channel it arrived by.The intake gateway acknowledges as it normalises; no person is needed. The received timer bounds the time to planning.
Plan and envelopeA routine task gets its plan summary and envelope proposal within the same sitting; a complex one says when to expect it.The planning timer; a task that overruns it appears on the board.
Step responseA ready card is picked up within the shift that issued it and closed within its step timer, unless the counterpart is closed.Routing by tier; the step timer; the handover rule that no card is left running on an empty seat.
Nudge cadenceA task waiting on the user is nudged visibly, each nudge further apart than the last, and then lapses rather than lingering; lapsed is never withdrawn.The nudge schedule per task type, an illustrative default of three nudges then lapse, on awaiting-authority, awaiting-user and awaiting-verdict; every nudge is a recorded event on the task's timeline.
Escalation windowA step blocked by a third party waits exactly as long as the party's stated response window and then escalates to a re-plan or a new route.blocked-third-party carries the window; awaiting-third-party carries the timer; expiry triggers escalate-resume.
Dual-control latencyA step in checking is checked within the shift that raised it.The L3 seat on every shift; a step past its checking timer goes to the board.

Two levels are deliberately absent. We make no promise about how long a task takes end to end, because the longest part of most high-consequence tasks is an institution's own clock, which we can chase but not set. And we make no promise per worker, because the unit the user experiences is the task and the unit the floor manages is the step. The pilot in 06 is where the words above acquire numbers.
