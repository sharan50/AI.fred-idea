1The alias model

A real value crosses five boundaries between the user's hands and an institution's, and no person on our side stands inside any of them. This section follows a value along that path, boundary by boundary, and states what may exist on each side. Figure 3.1 draws the zones; section 2 follows one placement; sections 3 and 4 specify the two boundaries most likely to leak. The named paths by which a person could see a value, and their closure, are gathered in the threat model. The customer's one-page version is the trust story.

1.1Alias types, classes and their policy

An alias is a typed name, never a disguised value. It is written {{name}} or {{name.qualifier}}: lower-case letters, digits and hyphens, with at most one dot; the name is the type and the qualifier tells instances apart ({{card.primary}}, {{account.hdfc}}). An alias is unique to one user and stable across tasks, so the same string on two users' task cards names two values and carries no character of either. The vault maps it to the current version of the value: a reissued card becomes a new version behind the same alias, and the ledger records that as custody, not as a use. The vault is a bought tokenisation service of the Skyflow, VGS and Basis Theory class, hosted in the region the user's market requires; DR-008 fixes buy over build and the vendor is open in 07, so nothing here asserts what any provider offers to-verify. Every alias belongs to one class, and the class is its whole policy: which surfaces may receive it, which partial forms the maskers must expect and cover, and whether an echo from the third party is normal. Class, surface and jurisdiction together are one lookup in the policy layer; a request outside the table closes the step refused-policy and nothing is placed.

Partial forms are masked as strictly as full ones, because a last-four beside a card that names "Client 4471" is enough to join a value to a person. Where a step must tell two instruments apart, the vault issues a partial under its own alias, {{card.primary.last4}}, and nothing on our side ever cuts a value; the ceiling for that is the strictest rule we operate under, UIDAI's requirement that an organisation processing Aadhaar display no more than its last four digits verified [1]. The never-ours class is a refusal, not a gap: the RBI cautions the public against sharing logins, KYC copies, card details, PINs, passwords or OTPs with any person or agency verified [2], and those acts bounce to the user's device as the passports section specifies. Beside the value, the vault record carries the alias's policy fields: type, class, permitted surfaces, the identity-act type its use implies, the residency region, how and when it entered, its version, and whether the user confirmed it. The policy layer and the envelope decide a use at runtime; the substitutor enforces that decision at the moment of use.

1.2Boundary one: the three doors a real value enters by

Every real value enters by one of three doors, and each door tokenises before anything else on our side sees it. The first is the user's own input. The intake gateway's first stage is the vault's edge tokeniser: the message body and every attachment go to the vault before parsing, under the three detectors 02 specifies (identifier patterns, known-value digests, the user's own declaration), and what continues is the message with aliases in place of values and document aliases such as {{doc.pan-card}} in place of uploads. For values we know we need we do not ask in chat: the user's device shows a form whose fields post straight to the vault, and we receive the alias. Closure: no raw body is logged or queued; the model runtime carries a second, separately deployed detector that refuses to plan on a candidate value and returns the message for re-tokenisation; the task card is generated from the tokenised request and screened again before it reaches a console; and the user's channel history stays on their device and the channel provider's servers, which are not ours.

The second door is a passport adapter. A rail returns real values by design, so each adapter's response handler runs inside a tokenising boundary: the payload is written to the vault before it is parsed, against a declared schema in which sensitivity is stated per field and never inferred, and an undeclared field is sensitive until it is classified. The adapter emits aliases plus non-sensitive attributes (document type, issuer, dates, whether the name matched). Closure: no payload log, network egress to the rail and the vault only, tokenise rights and never detokenise rights, and a schema reviewed with the market's legal pack so a new rail cannot leak by omission.

The third door is a third-party echo: a confirmation page repeating a card number, a voice system reading an account number back, a reply quoting it. It opens on the surfaces and is closed there, by maskers that run before any frame, audio segment or message reaches a person (section 3). A value we already hold is recognised by keyed digest and replaced with its alias; a new one, such as an account number the institution issues, is tokenised into a new alias with a ledger line. Recognition never compares against the value itself: the vault emits, through the hash service, a keyed digest of each value in every normalised form the class table names, the key lives in the hash service and rotates with the digests, and the maskers digest every candidate window of the same length in whatever they scan. Short values such as a date of birth are weak against guessing, which is why the maskers also mask by coordinate and by field type, why the digest set never leaves the real-value zone, and why the hash service holds no detokenise right.

1.3What exists on each side

Boundary two is the edge of the alias zone, the widest boundary in the design: the planner and its model runtime, the classifier, the policy flags, the worker console, the task cards, the context store, the logs, the access ledger, the QA and support tools and the evidence store all live inside it and hold aliases and vault-issued partials only. The vault's client library is not installed in any of them, none holds a credential the vault would accept, and network policy denies them the vault's endpoints and the substitutor's inbound channel. Across that boundary lies the small region where a value may exist: the vault, at rest and regionally hosted; the substitutor, in memory for one placement; a surface, transiently, in a field, on a call leg or in an outbound message at the relay; and the evidence pipeline, in memory until redaction is verified. The accent outline in Figure 3.1 is that region's edge. Only dots, tones, aliases and redacted evidence cross it outward, and one more thing: the ledger line the substitutor writes for every use, which the user reads on their own device.

1.4What each ledger line has to be able to say

"Placed by the system; no person saw it" is not a promise about culture. It is a property of the component that placed the value, of the stream the worker watched and of the pipeline that kept the evidence, and every claim that recurs in the ledger maps to the property that makes it true. "Placed by the system" holds because exactly one non-human component can detokenise, and every placement it makes is bound to a step, a card and a policy check, so a line is never written for a placement without a step. "No person saw it" holds because a worker reaches every surface through a rendered stream that the masker clears before delivery, raw frames exist only inside the accent boundary of Figure 3.1, and the console has no channel that carries a raw frame. "The worker's audio was muted" holds because the mute is applied by our media server on the leg between the far party and the worker, never by the worker's client, and the line is written from the server's own mute record and never from a worker's attestation. "In our browser" and "on our phone line" hold because the site controls in 04 keep a worker's own tools out of the room. "Masked before any person saw it" holds because the store accepts an artefact only with the verifier's signature over its hash (section 4). A line the system cannot make true by construction is a line the system is not allowed to write.
