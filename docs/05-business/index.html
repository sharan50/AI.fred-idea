1Why this page exists now

The previous edition deferred economics until after a Series A or B. That position was wrong and has been withdrawn. A seed process asks for the shape of the unit economics before it asks for anything else, and a plan whose cost per task might exceed its price per task cannot be tested by waiting. The engine is still unchosen, but an engine is a set of parameters, and a model built on parameters tells you which capability differences are worth paying for. That is the order this page now works in.

Everything below rests on informed priors, not on customers. The company has none yet.

Assumptionassumption Every number on this page is modelled rather than measured. The simulation, its scenarios and its limits are described on the model page, which names the run, the seed and the date the figures are quoted from; the instrument that replaces the priors is the measurement protocol, which is thirty tasks and about a week of work.

2The task model

Every task resolves into one of five outcomes, and the mix is what sets the cost base.

Bounced is deliberately not folded into human-led. It costs almost no labour and therefore barely moves headcount, but it is the outcome that spends the product promise, because the user is handed the task back. It is a product metric, not a cost metric.

3The one ratio that decides everything

Delivery cost scales with usage, not with users. The governing number is therefore human minutes per user per month, which is the product of promised task volume and blended handling time, set against average revenue per user.

At 158 minutes a month against a $37 average there is no scale at which the arithmetic recovers, because a generalist minute fully loaded costs roughly Rs 7 to Rs 16 depending on seniority mix and year, and the subscription buys only a few of them. At 59 minutes against a $64 average the business is a normal, capital-light services-plus-software company. assumption

Four parameters move the ratio, and they are the four the plan commits to. The run that shows what they are worth moves six others as well, which is set out in section 5, because the gap between the two configurations should not be read as though all of it were planned.

4The cost split

At 1,000 users on the earlier and now superseded promise of two tasks a day, the split of delivery cost ran labour 73 per cent, tokens 24 per cent, everything else 3 per cent. Figure 5.1 is the wider picture, the four blocks of the cost base month by month, in which delivery labour is one line among four; payment fees and make-goods are charged on revenue and sit outside it. assumption Two things follow and both are durable.

First, the intuition that tokens are the dominant cost is wrong at current prices. Token cost per task falls with model prices but rises with agent loop length and context size, and in the modelling those two roughly cancel for around two years. Tokens are never the largest line in any scenario.

Second, fixed overhead is trivial. Space and connectivity are about two per cent of cost, which is why the cheap-rent argument never moved the answer. The step costs that do move it are the foreign entities in section 8, and the floor that carries them, which is staged in DR-006 and priced as a step in 04.

One result is worth stating because it is easy to miss. In the previously specified configuration, the paths that end with the best cash position are the ones that grew slowly. A model in which growth is the thing that hurts you is the formal statement of negative unit economics.

Three qualifications belong with that table, and a reader who finds them for themselves will trust the rest of the page less. First, the planning line is an average taken within a family of paths, month by month, so it is not a path and its statistics are not a path's. The trough of an average is shallower than the average of troughs: the $1.95m above sits at the thirty-first percentile of what a path in this model actually needs, where the median path needs 24.5 crore, about $2.75m, the seventy-fifth percentile 39.6 crore and the ninetieth 57.8 crore, about $6.5m. assumption The round sizes in section 7 are set above the ninetieth percentile, so the plan is not under-raised; the trough is simply a smaller number than the one it is read as. The same arithmetic applies to the crossing in Figure 5.2: averaged lines cross at month 29, the median path at month 40.

Second, the configuration behind the right-hand column moves six parameters besides the four levers: a lower churn floor, a lower engineering headcount exponent, a lower tail handling floor, a faster approach to the automation ceiling, and the two foreign prices. Two of those carry real weight. Putting the churn floor back where the previous configuration had it costs 1.9 crore of peak cash and 4.6 points of the chance of ever turning profitable; putting the engineering exponent back costs 6.6 crore and 5.6 points, which is more than either automation lever is worth. assumption They are assumptions in the run and commitments nowhere, which is why both appear among the five measurements in section 10.

Third, the model prices a user and not a task: revenue is users times price, and the task allowance enters only as cost. The metered allowance of DR-024 therefore earns nothing in these numbers, and the tasks-per-user lever is modelled as a cost cap alone. That understates the lever and it means the commercial decision of the revision has not yet been tested by the instrument that is supposed to test it. Rebuilding the revenue side to price the tail is an open item in 07.

6How users arrive

This section exists because the model was interrogated and gave an uncomfortable answer. The rate at which users arrive in month 1 owns thirty-four per cent of the variance of the cash need, forty per cent of whether the business ever turns profitable at all, and sixty per cent of the month it crosses over. The thirty-one drivers on the delivery side, every handling time, automation share, token price, wage and seat cost among them, own between them under seven per cent of the first of those. assumption The page above argues about the ratio because the ratio decides whether the arithmetic can work. Growth decides whether it does.

What the model assumes is modest and should be said out loud. Users arrive as a fraction of the installed base each month, most likely eighteen per cent and between ten and thirty, decaying by a little under one per cent of itself a month, against a reachable Indian market between twenty-five thousand and two hundred and fifty thousand users at the tenth and ninetieth percentiles, starting from two hundred users in month 1. Each arrival costs between Rs 600 and Rs 2,200 to acquire, rising a little over one per cent a month. assumption Those are priors, and unlike the handling times they are not scheduled to be measured by anything currently planned.

Two consequences follow. The first is a threshold. Holding everything else at its distribution and pinning the arrival rate, half the paths never turn profitable within sixty months at about thirteen per cent a month, and nine in ten do at about seventeen. assumption The model assumes eighteen, which is above the cliff but not far above it, and the distance between the assumption and the edge is four points of a number nobody has observed.

The second is that growth changes sign with the unit economics. In the previously specified configuration, moving the arrival rate from its bottom tenth to its top takes the peak cash need from 96 crore to 199: growth is the thing that hurts you, which is what negative contribution per user means. With the four levers the same move takes it from 52 crore to 13. assumption Growth is therefore not a lever to pull until the ratio of section 3 is established by measurement, and a plan that spends on acquisition before then is spending to lose money faster.

There is also a number in this publication that does not reconcile, and it is better named here than found later. The geography model assumes a cost to acquire of a median Rs 1,150 in India, Rs 8,500 in the United Kingdom and Rs 11,200 in the United States, which at 89 rupees are about $13, $95 and $126. The partner review at 08 records a consumer fintech benchmark of US$1,340 to $2,140, marked to-verify there. The two ranges do not overlap at any point: the benchmark is eleven to seventeen times the model's United States median. assumption Part of the gap is fair, because the benchmark is a paid-acquisition figure for consumer fintech, and this model's growth is referral-led at a $52 and $105 price; the review set it against a $149 monthly price of its own estimating, which is not a price this record has ever carried. Part of it is not, and the part that is not is the difference between a business that grows by word of mouth and one that buys its users. Which of those AI.fred is has not been established, and until it is, every trajectory on this page rests on the cheaper of the two.

What this record does not have is a channel. No acquisition route is chosen here, no cost per acquired user is evidenced, and the model cannot help: acquisition cost is charged for every user who arrives, but no spend causes one to arrive, so the model can neither buy growth nor price the attempt. The consequential tasks are the hook, as 00 argues, and a hook is not a channel. Both the channel and the model's missing mechanism are open items with owners and triggers in 07.

In the viable configuration Series B is genuinely optional: the planning line needs no capital after month 36.

8Geography

Purchasing power is the argument for expansion and it holds: a US user paying $105 costs the same to serve as an Indian user paying $52, because the delivery floor is in Bengaluru either way. assumption

Three things qualify it. Acquisition cost tracks competition for attention rather than purchasing power, running ten times the Indian figure against a two times price difference. Staff serving those hours carry a night and language premium. And automation share is worse abroad at entry, because the workflow library is built against Indian institutions.

The decisive finding is about timing rather than principle.

9The commerce layer

The company already executes purchases the user asked for. Routing them through channels that pay a take rate is a margin on the existing business rather than a new one, worth roughly 1.2 per cent of routed spend once the share of transactions with a payable channel is allowed for. It reaches around twelve per cent of revenue by month 60, but because it is close to pure margin it more than doubles contribution at month 36. assumption It needs merchant leverage rather than users, which arrives somewhere around four to seven thousand subscribers.

It is constrained by one rule that is not negotiable: a merchant may never pay for placement in a recommendation. The moment they can, the assistant stops being the user's agent, which is the property the subscription is bought for. The rule is recorded in DR-024 and the withdrawal of data sold as an asset is in DR-019.

10What replaces this page

Nothing here is evidence. Five things must be measured, and the order below is the model's own rather than this record's intuition, which had the first of them missing entirely. They are: the rate at which users arrive and what it costs to make them arrive, which moves the answer further than every driver on the delivery side put together; minutes per user per month; twelve-month churn on a paying cohort; willingness to pay above $50; and whether the engineering headcount exponent falls as the workflow library generalises. assumption The first is the subject of section 6 and has no instrument yet, which is the largest hole in this page. The second is answered by the measurement protocol, which is thirty tasks and about a week of work. The third and fourth need a pilot cohort and a priced waiting list. The fifth is visible only over time, in whether the tenth institution costs less to add than the second. All five are open items with owners in 07.

AI.fred is a personal AI that builds your context, task by task, until it can act in your personal life the way someone who has known you for years would. It handles the ordinary things, a table, a return, a rental search, a trip, and it is built so that it can also be trusted with the ones you dread: the bank, the visa, the claim, the admission. The dreaded tasks are why people come. The ordinary ones are why they stay, and why the context compounds fast enough to be worth anything. AI.fred is a working title. This publication is a design record, not an offer.
