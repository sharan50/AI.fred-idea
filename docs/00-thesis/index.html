00Thesis

AI.fred wins where the flat-fee errand players cannot follow: the tasks that touch a customer's money, identity, institutions and health, where a leak or an unwanted act costs something real. Every rival is built so that a person or a model sees whatever the customer sends; we are built so that nobody on our side ever sees a real value and nothing irreversible fires outside an envelope the customer set, and that architecture, not a price or a channel, is the moat.

1Customer and wedge

The customer is a person with institutions to deal with and no time to deal with them. The wedge is the small share of their tasks that carry real consequence, because that is where no existing service can safely go, and where a customer pays close attention to who is holding their affairs.

1.1The customer

Picture the tasks rather than the demographic. A bank account to open in a second city; a know-your-customer refresh the bank keeps sending reminders about; an insurer to chase for a claim; a passport or visa renewal with an appointment nobody can get; a parent's hospital admission with three forms and a payment; a lease to sign, a tax notice to answer, a school place to confirm. Each of these needs someone to act as the customer: present a document, state an identity number, answer a security question, pay, commit. Today the customer does it in stolen hours, hands it to a relative or a trusted employee, or drops it. The second and third are the interesting ones. Handing a PAN card, a bank login or a one-time passcode to a driver, an accountant's clerk or a cousin is a leak with a friendly face, and a dropped task has a cost that arrives months later as a lapsed policy or a frozen account.

Our customer is the urban professional or family, in India's large cities and in the US and the UK, for whom these tasks come in a steady stream and whose affairs increasingly span more than one country. They already delegate; the flat-fee concierge players exist because of it. What they cannot yet delegate safely is the part that matters. We serve that part across six verticals carried in from the founder's prior work: banking and wealth; legal and administrative; identity and security; health and medical; family affairs; travel.

1.2What consequence means

A task carries consequence when one of two things could go wrong and could not be put right. The first is a person on the operator side seeing a value they should not: an identity number, a card, a password, a diagnosis. Once seen it cannot be unseen, and the exposure outlives the task. The second is an act the customer did not want that cannot be undone: money moved, an application submitted, a booking cancelled, terms accepted. We test every step against one question, the point-of-no-return test: can we return the world to its prior state by our own action, at no cost to the user and without a third party's consent? If not, the step is irreversible. These are the two fatal failures the whole design is built around; the index states them and how each is made structurally impossible.

Below the line those two failures draw sit errands and bookings: a table, a cab, groceries, a gift, a laundry pick-up, an itinerary to research. A wrong table is an annoyance. Above the line sit the verticals, where a wrong transfer, a leaked Aadhaar number, a missed filing deadline or a hospital form with the wrong consent is a harm. Figure 0.1 maps the field onto that line.

1.3How we cross it

The AI is the brain and the humans are hands. Every request becomes an ordered plan; a classifier marks each step as one the AI can run, one a worker can run from a task card, or one only the user can do on their own device. Workers, the AI and almost all of our software handle aliases such as {{pan}}; the real value lives in a bought vault and is placed into a form, a phone line or an email by one small isolated component at the last boundary, so that what the worker sees is a field of dots and what they hear is a muted line. Every task carries an authority envelope: a spend cap, the parties we may contact, the acts we may commit the user to, the identity acts they delegate. Delegation is total inside it and impossible outside it. And every use of every alias is written into a plain-words access ledger the customer can read:

  1. 14:32{{pan}} used to fill HDFC Bank's account-opening form, in our browser. Placed by the system. No person saw it.
  2. 14:40{{dob}} spoken to Max Healthcare's booking line, on our phone line. Placed by the system. The worker's audio was muted.
  3. 14:41A worker saw the form with {{pan}} shown as dots.

Those lines are the product. The trust story says it in a page a customer could read; the threat model in 03 names every path by which a person could see a real value and closes each one.

2Why now

Now, because the three things a consequence-bearing assistant needs have all arrived since the middle of 2025, and nobody who holds one of them holds the others: models that can plan and act; rails and rules that permit a delegate to act within an explicit, bounded grant; and a customer who has been taught to delegate at all. Table 0.2 carries the evidence, each claim with its status.

2.1The labs have found the ceiling

The frontier labs spent 2025 shipping consumer agents and 2026 withdrawing them. Table 0.2 records the sequence: Operator, ChatGPT agent mode, Atlas, Instant Checkout and Project Mariner have each been closed or folded into products that read pages and draft documents but hand the keyboard back at a login or a purchase, and the survivors are trained to refuse bank transfers, to watch rather than act on financial sites, and to advise against legal, medical and financial pages altogether. That is not a failure of the models. It is the labs discovering the wedge from the other side: a model working alone inside a user's own browser has no hands for the phone call or the counter, no surface it controls, and no way to bound what a hostile page can make it do. The planning is now good enough. What was missing was the harness around it, and the harness is what we build.

2.2The rules are moving toward bounded delegation

In every market we serve the direction of regulation is the same: delegation is being permitted where it is explicit and bounded, and liability is landing on whoever deploys the agent. Table 0.2 shows the pieces. In India, NPCI has admitted software profiles to delegated UPI payment only within per-transaction and monthly limits and only where each debit follows an explicit user action, has piloted agentic payments with the labs on pre-authorised limits, and is reported to be preparing a protocol for agents on UPI; the RBI now requires a factor unique to every transaction and holds the bank liable when its authentication fails. In the UK, the competition regulator has said a business is responsible for its agent whoever built it, and the Treasury has opened a payments consultation that covers agentic AI. In California, a deployer can no longer argue that the AI acted on its own. Each of these rewards a service that can show, per task, what authority it held and what it did with it, and each punishes a service that runs on a customer's shared credentials. We are designed around exactly that showing: the envelope is the grant, the ledger is the record.

2.3The customer has been taught to delegate

The flat-fee players are our top of funnel. A wave of Indian concierge start-ups has made a WhatsApp message to a human assistant a normal way to get a table booked or a document collected, and incumbents from Swiggy to Jio to Google are normalising delegation of the low-value task at little or no marginal effort (Table 0.2 and Table 0.1). None of them has moved up the consequence axis. The customers they have trained will arrive at the line with the habit formed and the sensitive task still in their own hands.

2.4The trust layer can be bought

The last input is a component we do not build. Tokenisation vaults with regional hosting are a product category, and the decision to buy that layer rather than build it is fixed in DR-008; which vendor, and whether it can hold Indian residents' values in India and UK residents' in the UK or EU, is an open item in 07. Buying it moves the hardest part of the guarantee, keeping real values out of our own software, onto a supplier whose whole business is that guarantee, and leaves us to build the parts nobody sells: the planner, the substitution component, the envelope, the state machine, the worker console and the ledger. The build-versus-buy table in 06 draws the line component by component.

3Markets

India is where we build and first operate; the US and the UK are where the commercial case is strongest; one core serves all three, and expansion is an adapter, a legal pack and a shift. That is a fixed decision, recorded in DR-007, and this section gives the reasoning behind it.

3.1India as the build base

Three properties make India the right place to build and to run the floor. The engineering depth is there: the labs themselves are building in Bengaluru, and Anthropic opened an office there in February 2026, naming India Claude's second-largest market verified [26]. A large English-speaking workforce means an operations floor whose day shift covers India and whose two night shifts cover the west, in one office under one set of site controls; the shift design in 04 draws the wheel. And India Stack is the richest set of consented rails anywhere for the acts our verticals need: DigiLocker for documents, the Account Aggregator framework for financial data, UPI Circle and Reserve Pay for bounded delegated payment. Those rails are gated. DigiLocker releases a document only after the user authorises the requester in their own session verified [27], and only an entity regulated by a financial-sector regulator may pull data through an Account Aggregator verified [28]. That shapes the adapter, which reaches the rails through the user's own device or a regulated partner and never around them, but it does not shrink the ceiling. India is also where the regulators have drawn the safest line in the plainest words, which section 4.3 returns to.

3.2The US and the UK as commercial ground

Willingness to pay for delegation is established in both countries: a human-assisted assistant sector has existed for a decade and premium concierge is a listed business (Table 0.1). The institutions are numerous, slow and paper-bound, and increasingly reached through a single identity; the UK is consolidating government access on GOV.UK One Login and brought a digital-verification trust framework into force on 1 September 2026 to-verify. Liability is firming in a way that favours us: a business answers for its agent, and payment regulation is being rewritten with agents in mind (Table 0.2). The adapters differ from India's, Open Banking and identity rails in the UK, bank-data aggregation and card tokenisation in the US, and both are researched as to-verify items in the passports section of 03. Outside India we expect identity acts to bounce to the user's device until the per-jurisdiction flag table says otherwise; that table is also the lobbying agenda, line by line, each line naming what to ask a regulator to permit and what the customer gains.

3.3One core, three adapters

The planner, the classifier, the task cards, the alias vault and substitution, the envelopes, the state machine, verification, the worker console, the ledger and the intake gateway are one build and country-agnostic. Per country we add a passport adapter, a legal pack and a shift, and the expansion playbook in 06 repeats that triplet for every market after the first. The residency question is real and is designed for: Bengaluru staff handling UK and EU personal data is a restricted cross-border transfer needing contractual safeguards, which is one more reason the vault, with its regional hosting, is bought; the data-residency map in 03 sets it out. The channel is a per-market launch choice rather than an architecture commitment; that decision is amber and recorded in DR-001, with the channel-agnostic intake gateway fixed beneath it.

4The competitive landscape

Every player in the field executes either through chat plus people who see everything, or through a model inside the user's own browser and accounts. None has an execution surface of its own, a published masking policy for its staff, or a bounded grant of authority, and the sensitive wedge is either refused outright or advertised with no safeguard behind it. Table 0.1 lists the players with each claim's status. The sources were read as search excerpts because page reads were blocked in this edition; DR-013 records what "verified" means here and the re-read that is owed before circulation.

4.1India: Faff and its peers

The Indian field is a year or two old and already crowded. Read down Table 0.1 and it has one shape. Faff sells unlimited tasks for a flat monthly fee to an invite-only base in two cities and executes through assistants who call vendors and customer care. Hulp bills on coordination hours, gives each family a pod of three assistants and has a human review anything that books, pays or goes out. M, from Dunzo's co-founder, runs household routines over WhatsApp in a couple of hundred Bengaluru homes on a large seed round. Behind them sit Pokus, Aviha, Pinch and a long tail of kitchen and household managers. The incumbents have moved too: Swiggy opened Crew to the public in three metros, Google's AI Mode books tables in India, Jio has announced a free call agent for its subscribers, and NPCI is piloting agentic payments on UPI with Razorpay and the labs within pre-set limits. Notice the shape of all of it. It is low-value delegated commerce, not identity work.

Two facts define the field for our purposes. First, the sensitive wedge is being advertised without a trust architecture: Faff lists government and banking tasks, Crew lists Aadhaar and document updates, Hulp lists payments, healthcare and government documentation, and no vendor in the set publishes any limit on handling PAN, Aadhaar, bank credentials or one-time passcodes, nor any masking or need-to-know policy for its staff verified [29] [30] [31]. Second, the only published scope exclusion we found anywhere in the field is Faff's exclusion of business work verified [32]. The customer is being invited to hand over exactly the values the regulator tells them never to share.

4.2The US and the UK: a thinning field and a retreating one

The US flat-fee family assistants have thinned. Table 0.1 records Panasonic closing Yohana in the US and then in Japan, and Milo shutting in 2026. Duckbill survives as the main consumer hybrid: an AI triages and a US copilot executes, with the customer's card stored in the app and purchases executed up to a cap, and a stated rule that it cannot impersonate the user. Fancy Hands is the one service in either field that describes a design in which staff never see the card number, and it caps each payment and asks the user to approve every one. Magic and Time etc are hourly assistants working inside the client's own accounts, and Time etc's terms make the client entirely responsible for safeguarding their logins. In the UK we found no hybrid of AI and human operators launched in 2025 or 2026 at all to-verify: the entrants are WhatsApp assistants without human operators, human virtual-assistant firms, and the concierge incumbents Ten Lifestyle Group and Quintessentially adding AI to human lifestyle managers.

The labs' consumer agents are a separate column, and they have retreated; section 2.1 and Table 0.1 record the closures and what the survivors refuse. New entrants such as Instinct, Pally and Hark are raising very large rounds to act inside the user's email, screen and accounts, and Instinct's first weeks brought reports of plain-text email storage and a perpetual training licence over user data (Table 0.1). The pattern is the same as the human-assisted firms' with the person replaced by a model: the surface is the user's own, and the values pass through the vendor.

4.3What none of them can safely do

Set the two fields side by side and the same two postures appear. The first is refusal: Yohana barred logins, account creation and medical, financial or legal work; Duckbill will not impersonate the user and refers legal and accounting work out; the labs' agents refuse bank transfers, block or advise against financial, legal and medical sites, and hand logins and final purchases back to the user. The second is exposure: a card held in the app and spent up to a cap; logins handed to a named assistant through a password manager; a WhatsApp thread in which a pod of assistants reads whatever the customer sends, Aadhaar included. Only Fancy Hands describes a design in which staff never see the payment value, and it is confined to small purchases with a per-payment approval. Nobody in either field describes an execution surface of their own, a redaction pipeline for evidence, a per-task grant of authority, or a ledger of what was used and who saw it.

The regulators have drawn the line for them. In banking, the RBI's liability framework leaves the full loss with a customer whose own negligence, such as sharing payment credentials, caused it verified [61], and the RBI has told the public not to share logins, KYC documents, card details, PINs or OTPs with any unidentified person or agency verified [62].

In identity, UIDAI tells Aadhaar holders never to share an Aadhaar OTP, to use masked Aadhaar, and reminds organisations to display only the last four digits verified [63]; the Aadhaar Act makes unauthorised disclosure of identity information an offence punishable by imprisonment verified [64]. In the UK the competition regulator has placed responsibility for an agent on the business that uses it, and in the US a standing authorisation to an agent has no home yet in Regulation E (Table 0.2). A service whose operating model is "send us your details and we will sort it" is asking the customer to be negligent in the regulator's own terms, and is accumulating the liability itself.

5The moat

The flat-fee players cannot follow us up the consequence axis because the property that makes their model work, a generalist who sees everything and can therefore do anything, is the property that makes the wedge unsafe. The trust architecture is not a feature they can add. It is a different company with a different promise, a different floor and a different liability posture, and it is visible to the customer in every ledger line.

5.1Why the flat-fee model stops at the line

Unlimited tasks for a flat fee is only possible when any assistant can pick up any task with whatever the customer has sent, in a chat thread, on the assistant's own phone, using the customer's own logins. That is a leak path by design and a path to an unwanted act by design: there is no envelope because there is no boundary to draw one on. A vault bolted on afterwards changes nothing, because the value has already been read by a person before it could be tokenised. Closing the paths means owning every execution surface, running the floor in an office under site controls, redacting every screenshot and recording before storage, and replacing "we do anything" with "we do these acts, inside this envelope, and we bounce the rest to your phone". Each of those cuts against the model that made them cheap. The regulators' lines then turn an exposure posture into a liability: the customer who hands over credentials is negligent in the RBI's terms, and the business whose agent acts is answerable in the CMA's.

5.2Why the labs' agents stop there too

The labs have the models and not the hands. Their agents act inside the user's own browser and accounts, so every sensitive value passes through the model and the vendor, and a hostile page can steer the agent; the labs' own measurements of that are why they advise against sensitive sites (Table 0.2). They have no telephony, no counter, no human for the step that needs one, and no grant of authority beyond a confirmation prompt. Their consumer surfaces have also churned within months, which is no foundation for a service that must answer for a filing or a transfer. We buy their models and put them inside a harness they have not built.

5.3What the moat is made of

Eight structural properties, each of which closes a path a rival leaves open, and each of which is specified for an engineer in 02 and 03:

  1. Aliases everywhere. Humans, the AI and almost all of our software see {{pan}}, never the value; the value lives in a bought vault (the alias model).
  2. Substitution at the last boundary by one small isolated component, into a browser we run, a phone line we own or a mail relay we operate; the worker sees dots and hears silence (the substitution component).
  3. Execution surfaces that are ours. A worker never uses their own phone, browser or email.
  4. Evidence redaction as part of the harness: no raw screenshot, recording or transcript is ever human-viewable, because the evidence system is itself a leak path (DR-009).
  5. An authority envelope per task with the point-of-no-return test on every step: irreversible and outside the envelope bounces to the user's device; irreversible and inside it runs under dual control (DR-004).
  6. A task state machine in which user abandonment is legitimate and system forgetting is impossible: no task reaches a terminal state without an explicit transition (DR-005).
  7. An office-mandatory 24/7 floor whose site controls are part of the guarantee, with need-to-know task cards that name a pseudonymised client and only the fields the step needs (DR-006).
  8. A plain-words access ledger, per task, of every use of every alias and who saw what, which makes all of the above visible to the customer (the ledger specification).

Copying one of these is easy. Copying all eight is building us, and it cannot be done on a customer base and an operations culture that were assembled on the premise that the assistant sees everything.

5.4What the moat is not

It is not the model: we buy models, and the labs will keep improving them for us. It is not the channel: the front door is a per-market launch choice, amber in DR-001, above a channel-agnostic gateway. It is not price: economics is deferred by the founder's decision, and this thesis carries no number of ours because it needs none (05). And it is not a claim that an incumbent could never build it. Swiggy, Jio or a bank could in principle. What Table 0.1 shows is that every incumbent move so far runs the other way, toward low-value commerce inside their own apps, and that none has committed to a human floor under site controls or to a liability posture for identity acts. If one of them publishes a masking architecture, surfaces of its own and a per-task envelope, this thesis narrows, and we will say so in 07 rather than pretend otherwise.
