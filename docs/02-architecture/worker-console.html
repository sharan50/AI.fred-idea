02Worker console

A worker sees exactly what one step needs and nothing else: a pseudonym, a script, aliases, an expected outcome and a result form. The console cannot render a real value because none reaches it. This page specifies that view, the codes a step closes with, dual control as the worker meets it, and the board that catches anything without a timer.

1The task card

A task card is the whole of a worker's world for the duration of one step, and it is generated, not written. When a human step reaches ready, the task service projects the step record from the step record in 02 into a card and offers it to the pool the step type names: L1 generalists, an L2 specialist pod for the vertical, or L3 super-trust for the step types the policy layer reserves, per the tiers in 04. A worker claims a card, the step moves to running, and the card is live until the step closes. Then it is gone: the console keeps no history a worker can open, and a worker never holds two live cards at once.

The card has six parts, in this order, and nothing else. A header naming the pseudonymous client ("Client 4471"), the task and step position, the pool, and the live step timer. The step, in one sentence. The script: what to say or do, in order, with the moments at which a value is to be placed marked as actions rather than as text. The fields for this step, as aliases, each with a Place control and a masked rendering slot. The expected outcome, which is the test the result is checked against. And the result form, which closes the step with one outcome code and the fields that code requires. Figure 2.5 shows the anatomy.

The Place control is the only way a value reaches a surface, and it never passes through the console. Pressing it sends the substitution component a request naming the task, the step, the alias, the surface and the target: a field selector in the managed browser, the call leg in telephony, an alias position in the mail relay. The substitution component checks the policy layer and the envelope, performs the placement directly into the surface, writes the ledger entry, and answers the console with "placed" or a refusal code. The console renders the answer in the masked slot; it has no route by which the value could arrive. If the request is refused, because the envelope does not cover the act or the jurisdiction flags it as bounce, the worker sees the refusal code and closes the step with refused-policy or blocked-outside-envelope; the task service does the rest.

Beside the card sits the surface panel: the masked video stream of the managed browser, the call panel with its mute indicator and its "value placed" notice, or the mail composer with aliases in the text. The card's timer is the step deadline from the state machine, and it is live in the sense the invariant requires: when it expires, the step does not wait for the worker; the task service records the expiry and re-queues, re-plans or escalates per the step type, and the card tells the worker so. How cards are pseudonymised, and what the floor around a worker looks like, is operational and specified in task cards in 04.

2The alias-only rule

A worker handles aliases only, and this is enforced by what the console is built from rather than by what workers are told. The rule has three layers: what a worker can see, what a worker cannot see, and what the console technically cannot render even if someone tried.

2.1What a worker can see

  • The pseudonymous client reference, the task and step position, the pool and the timer.
  • The step, the script and the expected outcome, in plain words, generated from the step record.
  • Each field the step needs, as a typed alias such as {{dob}} or {{pan}}, and the state of its placement: not yet placed, placed, refused.
  • The non-sensitive facts the step needs, as plain statements ("prefers morning appointments"), drawn from the context store by the planner and written into the step record.
  • The surface panel, masked: a browser stream in which every substitution point and every echoed value renders as dots, a call in which the placement window and any readback are muted, a mail draft that carries aliases.
  • The result form, the outcome codes and their required fields.

2.2What a worker cannot see

  • The user's name, address, phone number, email or any contact detail. The user is Client 4471 on every card, and a party we call is a party we dial from the envelope, never a number a worker reads.
  • Any real value, on any surface, at any time. Not in a field, not in a frame, not on the call, not in a draft, not in an artefact.
  • Any other step of the task, the plan, the envelope beyond the lines the step touches, the task's history or the user's verdict. A card carries the one line of context its step needs, and a worker who needs more closes the step with the code that says so.
  • The context store. A worker never queries it; the planner draws from it into the step record.
  • The evidence store. Redacted artefacts are for audit and dispute, and viewing them is a logged act by a person in a QA or escalation function, never a worker executing a step.

2.3What the console technically cannot render

The console has no vault SDK and no route to the substitution component's output, because that component never returns a value to any caller. Every record the console receives, the step record and the card payload, is checked at the task service before it leaves: a payload containing a string that matches a known-value hash is rejected and raises an incident. The browser stream is masked before it is encoded, inside the sandbox, so the unmasked frame never exists on the console's side of the wire. The audio the worker hears passes through the delay buffer specified in telephony in 02, which mutes a known value before it reaches the worker's ear; the placement window mutes the worker's audio in both directions. The mail composer holds aliases, and replacement happens at the relay after the worker is done. Copy, paste out, download, print and screenshot are disabled in the console, and the console runs on a locked station under the site controls in 04; the console is designed so that the site controls are the second line, not the first.

Two worker actions exist for the residual case. A third party who asks a worker to read out or confirm a real value is answered from the script: we do not hold it, and the system will place it; a worker who is pressed closes the step refused-policy. And a worker who sees anything that looks like a real value, a form echo the masker missed or a document rendered in the clear, presses one control: it blanks the surface panel, closes the step refused-policy with a leak report, and opens an incident. The path is named and closed in the threat model in 03; the console's job is to make the report one press away.

3Structured outcome codes

A step closes with exactly one code from the fixed vocabulary below and the structured fields that code requires, and the console will not submit a result until those fields are present. The vocabulary is the control loop specified in verification in 02: it decides what the task service does next, it is not a judgement of quality, and the user's verdict remains the only measure of that.

Table 2.5 The nine outcome codes, what each means and the fields the result form demands before it will close the step.
CodeMeaningRequired fields
okThe expected outcome was achieved.reference (if any), timestamp, party
ok-with-varianceAchieved, with a noted difference from the expected outcome (a fee, a date, a condition).as ok, plus variance note
blocked-third-partyA third party prevented completion for now (office closed, system down, callback promised).party, response window
blocked-needs-userA user-only act or fact is needed. Creates a user step.what is needed, why
blocked-outside-envelopeContinuing would exceed the envelope. Creates an extension request or a user step.the envelope line it would breach
failed-retryableA transient failure; the step can be re-attempted by the same route.error class
failed-finalThe step cannot be completed by this route. Triggers re-planning.reason, alternatives considered
refused-policyThe executor declined under policy (for example, asked to reveal a real value, or the policy layer prohibits the step type in this jurisdiction).policy reference
supersededThe plan changed and the step is no longer needed.superseding step

The form is shaped by the code. Choosing ok reveals the reference, timestamp and party fields, with the timestamp set by the console and the party pre-filled from the envelope; choosing blocked-third-party asks for the response window as a choice from the step type's allowed windows rather than a free number; choosing failed-final asks what alternatives were tried, as a checklist from the step type plus a free line. A worker cannot type a value into any field: reference fields accept the third party's reference, which is not ours to hide, and every free-text field is run through the same known-value check as a card payload before it is stored. superseded is never chosen by a worker; the task service applies it when a re-plan removes a step. Artefacts attach automatically from the evidence pipeline; a worker neither selects nor sees them.

4Dual control from the worker's side

For an irreversible step inside the envelope, whichever party executes, the other checks before it fires, and a worker meets this in two roles. As executor, the worker prepares the act from the card as usual, but the control that would fire it reads "Send for check" rather than "Fire"; pressing it moves the step to checking, freezes the prepared act, starts the check deadline and shows the worker a waiting state with the timer. The checker for a worker-executed step is the AI, in a separate model call that receives the act in one sentence, the envelope lines it touches, the expected outcome and the prepared evidence, masked, and no conversation history. It answers approve or decline with a reason. On approve the worker fires the act, or the surface fires it for them where the act is a submission, and the outcome record carries both identities. On decline the card shows the reason and the step returns to ready, or re-plans if the reason is that the route is wrong.

As checker, a worker receives a check card for an AI-executed step. It is smaller than a task card: the act in one sentence, the envelope lines it touches with the relevant values as aliases, the expected outcome, the prepared evidence masked, and two controls, approve and decline with a reason. A checker cannot edit anything; a check is a yes or a no against the envelope and the expected outcome, and that is deliberate, because a checker who can adjust the act becomes a second executor with nobody checking them. Check cards go to a pool the executing step's pool cannot draw from, and never to a worker who prepared any part of the step. A check card has its own deadline; a check that passes it goes to the exceptions board as a pending dual-control check, and the act does not fire in the meantime.

Two edge cases are handled by the task service, and the console shows their effect. If the user narrows the envelope while a step is in checking, the step is re-checked against the narrowed envelope before it fires; the checker sees a fresh check card marked as a re-check, and the executor's waiting state says why. If the user withdraws the task while a step is in checking, the step is cancelled before it fires and both cards close with the cancellation shown. The rule that a worker is checked by the AI and the AI is checked by a worker is recorded in DR-004; the mechanics on the system side are in dual control in 02.

5The exceptions board

The exceptions board is where the invariant is enforced by people, and it is a duty, not a dashboard. The watchdog specified in timers, nudges and the watchdog in 02 runs on a fixed cycle and places on the board every task that fails one of four checks. The board has four columns, one per check, and a card leaves a column only by an action that restores what was missing or by a recorded transition; there is no dismiss.

The columns and what clears them:

No live timer
A live task with no future-dated timer: the invariant's own condition. Cleared by a person assigning a timer of the right kind for the task's state, which is a recorded event; the board will not accept a timer in the past.
Dual-control check pending
A step in checking whose check deadline has passed or that has no checker assigned. Cleared by reassigning the check to a checker who then acts, or by the executor withdrawing the prepared act back to ready. The act never fires while the card is on the board.
Blocked past its window
A blocked-third-party step whose response window has passed without a response. Cleared by escalate-resume: a re-plan that produces a new route, or an escalation to an L2 pod or to the escalation function to chase the party, each of which sets a new window.
Nudge schedule exhausted
A task in awaiting-authority, awaiting-user or awaiting-verdict whose nudges are exhausted. The system will record the lapse; the card asks a person to confirm that no step is in flight and, for awaiting-verdict, that the outcome report was delivered, before the lapse event is written with the state it lapsed from. A user message at this point revives the task instead.

The board belongs to the QA and escalation function, with the shift lead accountable for it being empty at handover, as set out in QA and escalation in 04 and the shift design in 04. Every card shows who owns it and since when, and every clearing action is an event in the task's log, so the board's history is the audit of how silence was handled. A card cannot be hidden, snoozed or reassigned to nobody. The watchdog checks the board too: a card that has sat unowned for longer than its column's limit is escalated, and the limits are per column and illustrative until the pilot sets them.

6The invariant

The console is the place where a person is most tempted to leave a task for later, so the rule that forbids it is stated here in exactly the words used in the state machine in 02. The harness checks that the two are character for character the same.

A task never leaves the system by omission. Every task is in exactly one named state. Every non-terminal state carries a live timer. Every change of state is an explicit, recorded transition, and no task reaches a terminal state without one. Silence from the user is a legitimate way for a task to end: it moves through visible nudges into lapsed, and lapsed is not withdrawn. Silence from the system is forbidden: a task with no live timer is an exception, and the watchdog places it on the exceptions board until a person gives it one.

For a worker the invariant has three practical consequences. A card never closes without a code, so nothing a worker touches can end by being put down. A check never times out into an act, so nothing irreversible fires because someone was slow. And the board is a duty with an owner, so a task that has fallen silent is somebody's job by name until it is not silent. The decision behind the machine is DR-005.
